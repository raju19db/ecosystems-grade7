Interactions in the Environment

The environment is made of living and non-living parts that constantly interact with each other. Let's understand the big picture first!

🪨 Lithosphere
The solid mineral material in the biosphere, on Earth — basically all the rocks and soil.
The soil in your backyard, mountains, and the ocean floor are all part of the lithosphere.
💧 Hydrosphere
All the water in the biosphere, on Earth — oceans, lakes, rivers, ice caps, and groundwater.
The Pacific Ocean, the Amazon River, and even the water in clouds are part of the hydrosphere.
☁️ Atmosphere
The air in the biosphere that surrounds Earth — a blanket of gases including oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide.
The air you breathe right now is part of the atmosphere. Without it, life on Earth could not exist!
🌐 Biosphere
All the areas on and around Earth that can sustain life — where the lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere overlap.
From the deepest ocean trench to the highest cloud, anywhere living things exist is part of the biosphere.
🌿 Biotic
The living components of an ecosystem — anything that is alive or was once alive.
Trees, birds, mushrooms, bacteria, and even dead leaves are biotic factors.
🪨 Abiotic
The non-living components of an ecosystem, such as wind, sun, water, soil, and temperature.
Sunlight, rainfall, the temperature of the air, and the minerals in soil are all abiotic factors.
💡
Easy way to remember: Bio means "life" in Greek. So Biotic = living, Abiotic = NOT living. The letter "A" at the start means "without" — like "atypical" means not typical!

Levels of Life in an Ecosystem

Scientists organize living things into different levels — from a single living thing all the way up to the entire Earth!

🦔 Organism (Individual)
A single living thing — could be a plant, animal, fungus, or bacterium.
One hedgehog in a forest = one organism.
👥 Population
A group of organisms of the same species living in the same ecosystem.
All the hedgehogs living in Sherwood Forest = a population.
🌐 Community
A group of different populations interacting in the same ecosystem.
Hedgehogs + oak trees + foxes + mushrooms in Sherwood Forest = a community.
🌳 Ecosystem
All organisms living in a particular area, as well as all the nonliving physical components of the environment with which they interact.
A pond ecosystem includes fish, frogs, algae, water, mud, sunlight, and air temperature.
🌵 Biome
A large region of land with characteristic climate, soil, and organisms.
The Amazon Rainforest, the Sahara Desert, and the Arctic Tundra are each different biomes.
🔭 Niche
The role or "job" of an organism in its ecosystem — what it eats, where it lives, and how it behaves.
A woodpecker's niche is drilling into trees for insects — no other bird does this the same way.
🧠
Memory trick: Think of it like Russian dolls! Organism → inside a → Population → inside a → Community → inside an → Ecosystem → inside a → Biome → inside the → Biosphere.

Feeding Relationships

Energy flows through ecosystems as organisms eat and get eaten. Here's who eats what!

🌞 Producer
Plants (and some bacteria) that use energy from the sun to make nutrients they need to survive — through photosynthesis.
Grass, oak trees, algae, and wheat are all producers. They make the food that everything else depends on!
🍽️ Consumer
Organisms that eat food made by producers. Primary consumers eat plants; secondary consumers eat animals.
A rabbit (primary) eats grass → A fox (secondary) eats the rabbit.
🐄 Herbivore
An animal that eats only plant material.
Cows, deer, butterflies, and giraffes are herbivores. They are always primary consumers!
🦁 Carnivore
An animal that eats only other animals.
Lions, sharks, eagles, and owls are carnivores. They usually eat herbivores or other carnivores.
🐻 Omnivore
An animal that eats both other animals and plant material.
Bears eat berries (plant) AND fish (animal). Humans are omnivores too!
🦅 Predator
An organism that catches and eats other organisms of a different species.
A hawk swooping down to catch a mouse is acting as a predator.
🐭 Prey
An organism that is caught and eaten by another organism of a different species.
The mouse being caught by the hawk is prey. One organism can be both predator AND prey!
🦅 Scavenger
An organism that eats dead or decaying plant or animal matter.
Vultures, hyenas, and raccoons are scavengers. They clean up the ecosystem!
🍄 Decomposer
Organisms that break down the cells of dead or waste materials and absorb their nutrients, returning them to the soil.
Mushrooms, earthworms, and bacteria are decomposers. Without them, dead things would pile up everywhere!

Symbiosis

🔗 Symbiosis
An interaction between two organisms of different species living in close proximity that lasts over time. There are three types!
🦟

Parasitism

One partner is harmed, the other benefits.
(-/+)

A tick on a dog — the tick feeds on the dog's blood (tick wins, dog loses)
🌸

Mutualism

Both partners benefit.
(+/+)

Bees and flowers — bees get nectar, flowers get pollinated (both win!)
🐟

Commensalism

One partner benefits, the other is unaffected.
(+/0)

Barnacles on a whale — barnacles get transport, whale doesn't care
🧠
Quick memory trick:
Parasitism → Para means "beside" but also think "PARA" = one PARAsites, one suffers.
Mutualism → "Mutual" literally means "shared between both" — mutual benefit!
Commensalism → "Com" means together, "mensa" means table (Latin) — one eats at the table, the other doesn't notice.

Energy Flow & the Energy Pyramid

☀️ Energy Flow
The movement of energy, which originally comes from the sun, from one organism to another through a food chain or food web.
The sun gives energy to grass → a rabbit eats the grass and gets some energy → a fox eats the rabbit and gets some energy.

🔺 The Energy Pyramid

A pyramid showing the number of organisms at each level of a food chain. The base is wide (many producers), and each level gets smaller because only about 10% of energy passes to the next level. The rest is lost as heat!

🦅 Top Predator
🐍 Secondary Consumer
🐸 Primary Consumer
🌾 Producers (Plants)
⚡
The 10% Rule: When energy moves from one level to the next, only about 10% is transferred. The other 90% is used by the organism for its own life processes or lost as heat. This is why there are always FAR more plants than herbivores, and far more herbivores than carnivores!

Succession & Sustainability

🌱 Succession
The process by which new species gradually replace old species in an ecosystem over time. Think of it as nature's slow renovation!

Primary Succession — Starting from Scratch

Happens on bare rock with NO soil. Very slow! Takes hundreds of years.

🔥 Secondary Succession
When succession occurs where soil already exists — after a disturbance like a fire, flood, or human clearing. Faster than primary succession because the soil is already there!
After a forest fire, grasses and wildflowers quickly return because the rich soil is still there. Within decades, a forest can regrow!
♻️ Sustainability
The ability to sustain something — to keep it healthy and continue in the same manner. In ecology, it means using resources in a way that the ecosystem can continue to thrive for future generations.
Using solar energy instead of burning coal, sustainable fishing limits, and planting trees to replace ones cut down are all examples of sustainability in action.
